Now go back in time, to nineteen forty-four. You're nineteen, on a brutal battlefield in Italy.

Kamal Ram was born on the seventeenth of December, nineteen twenty-four, in Bholupura, a small village in Karauli, a district of Rajasthan. He voluntarily joined the army as a sepoy, a private soldier, in the Third Battalion of the Eighth Punjab Regiment.

So why should you know his name? Because from the Imperial War Museum in London to his memorial in Monte Cassino, Italy his bravery is remembered, and it all started with one night in nineteen forty-four.

On the night of the twelfth of May, nineteen forty-four, near Monte Cassino, Allied forces attacked the Gustav Line, one of the strongest German defences in Italy.

In the depth of night, Kamal Ram's company crossed the River Gari. Then the guns began to fire on them. Four German machine-gun posts, stopping the men in their tracks.

To progress, someone had to silence these guns. The young Kamal Ram volunteered.

He crawled forward. Closer. And closer.

He knocked out the first post, alone. 

Then the second. 

Then he helped destroy the third.

Even as a platoon, a group of his fellow soldiers, was fired on from a house, Kamal Ram ran straight at it, capturing the Germans soldiers there.

His courage helped secure a bridgehead: safe, tactical ground on the enemy's side of the river.

By morning, the guns were silent, and German soldiers had surrendered to a nineteen-year-old.

His bravery helped open the way for the Allied advance. And when King George the Sixth came to Italy, with the war still raging, he pinned the Victoria Cross on him in person on the twenty-sixth of July, nineteen forty-four.

Because he was just nineteen years old. Yet, even with four enemy guns ahead of him, he went forward anyway.
