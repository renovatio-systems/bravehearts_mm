# Yeshwant Ghadge

**Medal:** Victoria Cross (posthumous). Earlier Mentioned in Dispatches (1941). Memorial in Montone, Italy.

## Script

The gunfire stops. Naik Yeshwant Ghadge looks around.

Everyone in his section is gone. Killed, or pulled back. He's the only one left.

Ahead of him: a German machine-gun post, still firing.

Ghadge was born in nineteen twenty-one, in the village of Palasgaon, in Maharashtra. By nineteen forty-four, he was a naik, a corporal, commanding a small section of men in the Fifth Mahratta Light Infantry. Three years earlier, he'd already been mentioned in dispatches once, for an act of bravery that never made the history books either.

It's the tenth of July, nineteen forty-four, in the Upper Tiber Valley, north of Rome. He's twenty-two years old, and the decision is his alone.

A month earlier, Rome had fallen to the Allies. But the war in Italy was far from over. German forces had pulled back to new defensive lines further north, and the Allied advance had to keep pushing them back, valley by valley, hill by hill, towards the mountains that would become the Gothic Line.

This machine-gun post is one small part of that slow, grinding push. But right now, for Ghadge, it's the only thing that matters. If it isn't silenced, no one behind him moves forward.

He doesn't wait for orders. He doesn't wait for help.

Ghadge runs straight at the gun. Alone.

He reaches it. He silences it. The post is his.

But the Germans aren't finished. Snipers have him in their sights.

He's killed in the position he just captured.

His death wasn't the turning point of a campaign. It was one soldier's decision, in one small action, that let his unit keep moving forward that day, part of the same long push towards the Gothic Line.

For that single act, alone, with no one left beside him, Yeshwant Ghadge was awarded the Victoria Cross, posthumously.

There's a statue of him today, in Mangaon, in Maharashtra, where he was born. His name is carved on the Cassino War Memorial too, although his body was never recovered. No grave, just a name on a wall, and a village that still remembers him.

Ghadge went forward when there was no one left to go with him.
