# M.K. Unni Nayar

**Story angle:** Not a medal story — a protection story. In July 1944, Nayar's Maratha Light Infantry unit became the first Allied guard ever posted over hundreds of Uffizi masterpieces sheltered at Montegufoni Castle, outside Florence.

## Script

Imagine carrying the weight of their relics and artefacts, centuries of Western civilisation's memory, in a country you have never called home, while the war is still rolling past the door.

That was the job given to Indian soldiers in Italy in the summer of nineteen forty-four. They had come to fight, and some would die, to free Italy and the people of Europe from a tyranny that threatened all of them. Now they were charged with protecting what that freedom was meant to preserve.

Manakampat Kesavan Unni Nayar was born in nineteen eleven, in Kerala. He was a captain in the Maratha Light Infantry, serving in a land far from home, and he took the responsibility seriously.

On the thirtieth of July, nineteen forty-four, Captain Nayar travelled to Montegufoni Castle, about twenty-five kilometres outside Florence. He went with a BBC reporter, Wynford Vaughan-Thomas, and the novelist Eric Linklater, then a British army officer.

Inside, they found two hundred and seventy-two masterpieces, stacked against the walls. Paintings by Botticelli, including the Primavera. Works by Michelangelo.

Florence's museums had moved them there two years earlier, to keep them out of harm's way. Italian custodians and art officials had stayed with the collection through the war, caring for it when few others could.

But shelter was not the same as protection. The front line was close, and anyone could walk in.

So Nayar's unit posted a sentry at the castle gate. An Indian sergeant chalked two words on the doors: Out of Bounds.

It was the first Allied guard these paintings had ever had.

The visiting officers were so moved that they proposed something unusual. When the paintings returned to the Uffizi Gallery, one room should be named in honour of the Maratha soldiers who had stood watch over them.

Think about that. Soldiers far from home, guarding a heritage that belonged to the people they had come to liberate, so that people they would never meet could one day stand before it. They were thousands of miles from the villages and cities they knew, with no guarantee of ever returning to them, and still they kept the watch.

Those paintings still hang in Florence today. For a time, they depended on a handful of Indian soldiers, and on the Italians who had kept them safe for two years before that.

Unni Nayar. He guarded the art of the people he had come to free, and he did it with care.

## Fact-check notes
- Confirmed via the Comune di Firenze page: date (30 July 1944), location (Montegufoni Castle, ~25km from Florence), headcount (272 artworks), the "Out of Bounds" sentry detail, the "Maratha Room" suggestion, and that his unit stayed until 7 August 1944 and was visited by the 8th Indian Division's commander, General Russell.
- Script is careful to credit Nayar's unit with guarding the already-sheltered art, not rescuing or discovering it — the artworks were moved and cared for by Italian officials (custodian Guido Masti, Soprintendenza official Cesare Fasola) two years earlier.
- Nayar held the rank of Captain in 1944 (CSV's "Captain; later Colonel" is correct); confirmed date of death as 12 August 1950, Korea. Script does not use the death or Korea framing.
- Spelling discrepancy: the source gives Linklater's first name as both "Eric" and "Erik" — script uses "Eric."
- Removed from the earlier draft as unverified: the journalism training, the claim that he was known for his love of Italian art, and the visitors' book and battalion history details.
