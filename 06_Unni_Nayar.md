# M.K. Unni Nayar

**Story angle:** Not a medal story — a protection story. In July 1944, Nayar's Maratha Light Infantry unit became the first Allied guard ever posted over hundreds of Uffizi masterpieces sheltered at Montegufoni Castle, outside Florence.

## Script

You've probably seen a "do not touch" sign in a museum.

In the summer of nineteen forty-four, someone had to do more than that. Someone had to physically guard the art, with the front line still close by.

That someone was Indian.

Manakampat Kesavan Unni Nayar was born in nineteen eleven, in Kerala. Before the war, he trained as a journalist, and was known as someone who genuinely knew and loved Italian art, not just a soldier passing through.

On the thirtieth of July, nineteen forty-four, Captain Nayar travelled to Montegufoni Castle, just outside Florence, with a BBC reporter named Wynford Vaughan-Thomas, and a British army major who also happened to be a novelist, Eric Linklater.

Inside, they found two hundred and seventy-two masterpieces, stacked against the walls. Paintings by Botticelli, including the Primavera. Works by Michelangelo. Florence's museums had moved everything there two years earlier, to keep it safe from the bombing and the fighting, under the care of a custodian and an Italian art official who'd stayed with the collection the whole time.

But safe storage wasn't the same as protection. The front line was close. Anyone could walk in.

So Nayar's unit, soldiers of the Maratha Light Infantry, posted a sentry at the castle gate. An Indian sergeant chalked two words on the doors: Out of Bounds.

It was the first Allied guard the artworks had ever had, in a war zone where almost anything could still go wrong.

The visiting Englishmen were so struck by it that they suggested something unusual: that when the paintings went home to the Uffizi Gallery, one room should be named in honour of the Maratha soldiers who'd stood watch over them.

Nayar's part in all this never made him famous. There's no medal for it, no citation, nothing carved on a war memorial. Just a line in a visitors' book, and a battalion history, decades old, that most people will never read.

Just two hundred and seventy-two paintings, still hanging in Florence today, that briefly depended on a handful of Indian soldiers nobody back home had ever heard of.

Unni Nayar. The man who helped guard someone else's masterpieces.

## Fact-check notes
- Confirmed via the Comune di Firenze page: date (30 July 1944), location (Montegufoni Castle, ~25km from Florence), headcount (272 artworks), the "Out of Bounds" sentry detail, the "Maratha Room" suggestion, and that his unit stayed until 7 August 1944 and was visited by the 8th Indian Division's commander, General Russell.
- Script is careful to credit Nayar's unit with guarding the already-sheltered art, not rescuing or discovering it — the artworks were moved and cared for by Italian officials (custodian Guido Masti, Soprintendenza official Cesare Fasola) two years earlier.
- Nayar held the rank of Captain in 1944 (CSV's "Captain; later Colonel" is correct); confirmed date of death as 12 August 1950, Korea.
- Spelling discrepancy: the source gives Linklater's first name as both "Eric" and "Erik" — script uses "Eric."
